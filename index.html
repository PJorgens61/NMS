A menu bar app for your Mac

Before you restart the router — know what's actually broken.

NMS sits quietly in your menu bar and watches every layer between your Mac and the internet: the Wi-Fi, the router, DNS, the ISP's own edge, the sites you're trying to reach. The moment one of them fails, the dot tells you which — instead of you guessing.

"Is it me, or is it everyone?"

A call drops. A page won't load. Your team's Slack goes quiet mid-sentence. You don't have an IT department down the hall — you have a menu bar and a guess. NMS turns the guess into an answer, in the time it takes to glance up.

  • → Is your Wi-Fi actually ready for that Zoom call? Check signal strength and latency before you join, not after it drops.
  • → "Why does the Wi-Fi feel slow today?" — a real answer (weak signal, a saturated link, high latency), not a shrug.
  • → Also checks the status pages Slack, Google, Notion, and a dozen other SaaS tools publish themselves — so a real outage on their end never gets mistaken for a problem on yours.
  • → A quiet history of every outage, so "the internet's been flaky all week" stops being a feeling and starts being a timestamped fact.

2 field-technicians.lan 1.2 ms

Scope out a customer's network in a flash.

You show up to a site you've never seen. NMS sweeps the subnet, finds every switch, access point, and router that speaks SNMP, traces the real path out to the internet, and identifies the ISP on the other end of it — all before you check the ancient documentation.

  • → Detects what changed since your last visit — a restart, a firmware update, a device that wasn't there before.
  • → Every SNMP device's software version and system uptime, at a glance — with one click to access the device's web page.
  • → Recognizes each network by fingerprint and keeps its own history separate — DHCP leases included, so you see what actually got handed out, and this site never gets confused with the last one.

Break things on purpose. Just know when you actually did.

A new VLAN, a Docker network spun up at 2am, a router firmware flash that seemed fine — homelabs change constantly, and half the fun is finding out what breaks. NMS runs as a continuous smoke test in the background, so when something regresses, you know within a poll cycle instead of the next time you notice a device's gone quiet.

  • → A continuous smoke test of your own network — DNS, DHCP, the gateway, every device you've told it to watch — running the whole time you're editing configs.
  • → When you're done experimenting, know your network is actually back to normal — every check green again, not just "probably fine now."
  • → See exactly what changed right before something broke — a topology change correlated against the failure, not a coincidence you have to spot yourself.
  • → One more Mac watching, zero setup — no separate monitoring stack to stand up before you can trust it.

4 corporate-it.internal 3.8 ms

A quiet, always-on second pair of eyes.

NMS doesn't replace your monitoring stack — it sits on the machines closest to the actual problem, remembering every network they've ever joined, and correlating a slowdown against whatever changed right before it.

  • → Helps your remote users support their own home networks — they see their Wi-Fi is fine but the VPN gateway isn't, before it becomes a ticket in your queue.
  • → Flags when a failure lines up with a recent topology change, instead of leaving you to notice the coincidence yourself.
  • → No account, no cloud, no telemetry — everything runs on the Mac it's installed on. The source is fully open, so your security team can verify that directly instead of taking a vendor's word for it.

5 swift-programmers.dev 6.1 ms

Built the way you'd want a Mac utility built.

SwiftUI for the interface, SwiftData for local persistence, and nothing else — no third-party dependencies at all. Where macOS already ships a tool that does the job right, NMS shells out to it instead of reinventing it.

  • → Modern Apple technologies for the interface and logic — SwiftUI, SwiftData, Swift concurrency throughout.
  • → BSD tools doing the actual network work on the backend — ping, traceroute, snmpget, the same ones Darwin inherited from 4.2BSD networking.
  • → No dependencies. None. Not one third-party package.

Every decision, written down — including the honest parts.

This is a personal project, published so other people can try it. It's early. It works well against the network it was built on, and it says so plainly rather than pretending otherwise.

  • → MIT-licensed — read it, fork it, take the parts that are useful.
  • → No paid Apple Developer account behind it yet, so builds are ad-hoc rather than notarized — clone it and build it in Xcode.
  • → Bugs and rough edges are tracked in the open, not smoothed over in a changelog.

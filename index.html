"Is it me, or is it everyone?"

Before you restart that router — know what's actually broken.

A call drops. Your email won't load. Your team's Slack goes quiet mid-sentence. You don't have an IT department down the hall — you have a menu bar and a guess. NMS turns the guess into an answer, in the time it takes to glance up.

  • → Is your Wi-Fi actually ready for that Zoom call? Check your signal strength and networkQuality before you join, not after it drops.
  • → "Is the Wi-Fi slow today?" — a real answer (weak signal, dropped packets, power problems), not a shrug.
  • → Also checks the official status pages for the top SaaS apps — understand why your network is fine, but you can't run your apps.
  • → A quiet history of every outage, so "the internet's been flaky all week" stops being a feeling and starts being a timestamped fact.

2 field-technicians 1.2 ms

Scope out a customer's network in a flash.

You show up to a site you've never seen. NMS sweeps the subnet, finds every switch, access point, router, and printer that speaks SNMP, traces the real path out to the internet, and identifies the ISP on the other end of it — all before you can check the ancient documentation.

  • → Detects what changed since your last visit — a restart, a firmware update, a device that wasn't there before.
  • → Every SNMP device's software version and system uptime, at a glance — with one click to access the device's web page.
  • → Recognizes each network by fingerprint and keeps its own history separate — DHCP leases included, so you see what actually got handed out, and this site never gets confused with the last one.

3 network-builder 1.8 ms

Amazon just delivered a stack of boxes. How will you know when you're done?

Continuous monitoring from the moment each device comes online.

New router, new switch, new APs, cables everywhere. Plugging things in isn't the hard part — knowing you're actually finished is. NMS watches every piece as it comes up, so "done" is a verified state, not a guess you stop making once you're tired of running cables.

  • → Is DHCP actually handing out leases, and DNS actually resolving — before you plug in device #12 and start troubleshooting from scratch.
  • → Is your ISP service actually up, not just the lights on your new router.
  • → Can you actually reach the SaaS apps you depend on — not just "the internet," the specific things you need working today.
  • → Is it fast? A real speed test and bufferbloat check, not a guess based on the box the router came in.
  • → With Firewall Visibility configured, know the moment your new build changes what's reachable from outside — a forwarded port that used to work, or shouldn't, catching a misconfigured rule on the spot instead of weeks later.

4 homelab 2.4 ms

Break things on purpose. Know when they come back.

Experiment with your firewall. Try a beta version of that router software. Get the cables in your rack groomed perfectly. Homelabs change constantly, and half the fun is finding out what breaks. NMS runs as a continuous smoke test in the background, so when something regresses, you know in a flash that things aren't right.

  • → A continuous smoke test of your own network — DNS, DHCP, the gateway, every device you've told it to watch — running the whole time you're editing configs and moving cables.
  • → When you're done experimenting, know your network is actually back to normal — every check green again, not just "probably fine now."
  • → See exactly what changed right before something broke — a topology change correlated against the failure, not a coincidence you have to spot yourself.
  • → Watches your dynamic public IP and logs the exact moment it changes — the thing that silently breaks a self-hosted VPN endpoint or a port-forwarded/DNAT'd service (Plex, a game server, remote access into your lab) if you're not on a static IP and nothing's watching for the change. No more finding out your WireGuard peer's been unreachable for two days because the ISP handed out a new address.
  • → Runs Apple's own networkQuality test on demand to catch bufferbloat — the reason you get fragged in Call of Duty while watching a 4K Netflix stream. Know if it's the network, not your aim.
  • → A simple Mac app, zero setup — no separate monitoring stack to stand up before you can run it.

5 self-hosters 3.0 ms

Can't get there from here. Let NMS show you the way.

A real outside-in answer, not a router config you're just trusting.

You forwarded a port for Plex, a game server, or remote access into your homelab. Does it actually work from the internet, or just from your own couch? Firewall Visibility asks FW — a free, open, internet-hosted companion service, not run from your own network — to try reaching your current public IP from genuinely outside it, the same vantage point a friend three states away would have.

  • → Point it at a port you've forwarded and get a real answer: reachable from the outside, or not — not just "the router says it's configured."
  • → A device token, generated once and stored in this Mac's own Keychain — no account, nothing else about your network leaves this Mac.
  • → Runs on demand, or automatically alongside an SNMP-detected topology change — the moment your port-forward might have broken, you find out before your friends do.

6 path-discovery 3.4 ms

Lost in the woods? NMS can set you straight.

Ever wonder how your network connects to the world? See it now.

"The ISP" isn't one faceless blob — it's a real chain of routers, and Path Discovery draws it. It merges this Mac's own outbound trace with reverse traces run from several outside vantage points simultaneously (via Globalping, free and unauthenticated), so you see where every path converges on the same infrastructure, not just your own one-sided view of it.

  • → Confirms whether the "ISP Edge Router" hop really is your ISP's edge — a majority of outside vantage points agreeing is real evidence, not a guess.
  • → Every interface's DNS name and IP address, not just one — a single router can answer to more names than you'd expect.
  • → Vantage points aren't fixed at five, and aren't limited to the US — dial the count up or mix in other countries to see your path from further away.

8 swift-programmers 6.1 ms

Built the way you'd want a Mac utility built.

SwiftUI for the interface, SwiftData for local persistence, and nothing else — no third-party dependencies at all. Where macOS already ships a tool that does the job right, NMS shells out to it instead of reinventing it.

  • → Modern Apple technologies for the interface and logic — SwiftUI, SwiftData, Swift concurrency throughout.
  • → The actual backend is ping and traceroute — the same BSD tools Darwin inherited from 4.2BSD networking — plus dig for DNS lookups and Net-SNMP, the open-source SNMP toolkit with roots in Carnegie Mellon's early-90s CMU SNMP project.
  • →The bufferbloat/responsiveness test is Apple's own networkQuality — an implementation of draft-ietf-ippm-responsiveness, a draft IETF standard, not something we invented.
  • → No dependencies. None. Not one third-party package.

The gap

Windows has a hundred network apps. Mac doesn't have one — until now.

NMS is modern in every sense: built with Claude, by one person, for the problem people actually have today — flaky Wi-Fi, ISP handoffs, the SaaS your work runs on going down instead of your LAN. Much of what exists for Windows is from the before times — before Wi-Fi was the default, before "the internet" was the everyday point of failure, before an AI coding assistant made it possible for one person to ship something like this in weeks instead of years with an enterprise budget. This started as a Mac app because that's what the author uses, not because Windows doesn't deserve a modern one too.

  • → We'd genuinely welcome a Windows port — same idea, same honesty about what's actually broken, on the platform that's had a hundred network apps and never quite gotten this one.
  • → The problem NMS solves — flaky Wi-Fi, ISP handoffs, DNS/DHCP weirdness — is the modern home and hybrid-work network. A lot of what's out there for Windows still assumes the LAN-and-file-shares problem from a different decade.

The outside view

NMS sees your network from the inside. FW sees it from the outside.

Everything NMS shows you — DHCP, DNS, the path to your ISP — is a view from your own Mac, behind your own router. That's most of the picture, but not all of it: nothing on your side of the router can tell you what's actually reachable from the internet. FW is a small, stateless companion service NMS calls out to for exactly that — a real answer from the other side of the NAT, not a guess from inside it.

  • → See FW — no account, nothing remembered about the request once it's answered.
